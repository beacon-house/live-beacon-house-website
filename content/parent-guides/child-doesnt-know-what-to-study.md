Your child is 14 or 15. You ask what they want to study, and you get a shrug. Or a different answer every month. Or an answer that sounds borrowed: computer science, because everyone's parents work in tech.

This guide is for that moment. It covers what the shrug means, what we at Beacon House have seen work with hundreds of families, and what you have at the end.

## The short answer

Your child does not need a career at 14. They need an area of interest: a broad field, such as engineering, economics or biology, that can guide the next three or four years. We find it in four steps. Understand how your child is wired. List six to eight possible areas. Cut the list with light exposure to each. Then test the last two or three with small pieces of real work. A real interest lasts when the work gets harder. A passing interest fades.

## Not knowing is normal

Most children in Class 9 or 10 do not know what they want to study. When a parent tells us their child has no idea at all, our answer is the same almost every time: your child is exactly where we expect a child this age to be.

Parents often hear that successful children knew from the start. They did not. The world is too complex for a 14-year-old to decide what they will do for life. Nobody should ask them to.

So the aim is smaller than it sounds. Your child does not need a career. They need a working area of interest for the next three to four years. Enough to pick subjects, to choose what to spend time on, and to know what to say no to.

That is a different question from "what will you be". It is "what kind of problems do you find interesting?" Children can answer that one.

## Why marks and enthusiasm are not the answer

Three things get confused at this age.

Good marks in a subject. Liking a subject. Wanting to spend years on it.

They are not the same. A child can score well in physics because the school teaches it well. A child can say they love biology because a teacher was kind. Neither tells you whether they will stay with it when the work gets hard.

So we do not go by what a child says. We go by what they do. If your daughter says marine biology, what has she done about it? What has she read without being told to? Where does she spend her time when no one is watching?

Watch for the safe answer too. We see it often: computer science because the parents are in IT, medicine because it sounds secure, business because an uncle runs one. When we test these, some hold up. Many do not. One of our students told us he chose computer science partly because it impressed the people around him. Once he got a taste of the real work through our small projects, it was clear that computer science was not his interest. He moved on.

One more thing. Careers are no longer linear. Your child will change careers more than once, possibly by their 30s. The point of finding an area now is not to predict their life. It is to give the next four years a shape.

## Step 1. Understand how your child is wired

At Beacon House, we start with the child, not with universities.

The questions we ask parents are simple, and you can ask them yourself:

- What does your child do when no one asks them to?
- What do they avoid, however much you push?
- Where do they keep going on their own, without being reminded?
- What gives them energy, and what drains them?

We also use a set of diagnostic tests. They are useful, but we treat the results as a starting guess, not a verdict. More on that below. The tests give us maybe a third of the picture. The rest comes from conversation: open-ended, Socratic, over several sittings. Sometimes we give the child a problem to solve while we watch. How a child approaches a problem tells us more than any form they fill in.

Two things we look for: is this child drawn to pure ideas or to building things? And do they stay with a problem when they cannot solve it, or do they move on?

## Step 2. Make a long list

Once we have a sense of the child, we list the areas where someone with this wiring could do well. Usually six to eight. Not one.

The list includes the stated interest, fields next to it, and a few that look nothing like it. A child who says "biology" gets biology on the list. They also get biomedical devices, crop science, and something unrelated that fits how they think.

We build the list with one eye on what AI is doing to work. Some fields will shrink. Nobody can say which jobs will exist in ten years, so we do not chase trends. We start from what does not change: what your child is good at and what they care about.

## Step 3. Light exposure, then cut

Then the child dips a toe in each area. Videos, podcasts, reading, a short reflection after each. Where we can, a conversation with someone who works in the field and can push back.

For each area the child answers four questions. What does a normal day look like? What skills does it need? Do those skills match who I am? Would I enjoy it?

This is where the list shrinks. Some areas the child simply does not engage with. They watch the video and have nothing to say. Those come off the list. Usually six to eight areas become two or three.

## Step 4. Small experiments

A real interest lasts when the work gets harder. A passing interest fades. So the last step is a short piece of real work in each of the two or three areas left.

This is how we test it. We give the child problems in the area of interest that get harder each week, and they are not school-type questions. We are not checking whether the child gets them right. We are checking what the child does when a problem gets hard: tries again and asks for the next one, or puts it down and does not return to it.

The experiments are small. A few weeks each. A survey. A build. A short investigation. The question is not "did it come out well". It is "did the child want to keep going".

Sometimes the answer is no, and that is a good result. One of our students tried an engineering exercise with gears, circuits and CAD, and found she preferred the theory to the calculation. That told her something a test could not.

Here is what a set of experiments looked like for one Class 9 student testing behavioural economics. He wrote four short pieces, about two weeks each: why people do not always buy the cheapest option, how gold held its value over six years, how subscription businesses keep their customers, and why some luxury goods sell more when the price goes up. The feedback after each one was specific: back the argument with evidence, from published research or a survey of your own. By the fourth piece, he was asking the questions himself. The next area he tested was public policy, and it held his interest even more. That is where he is now building.

Sometimes a child shows no spark for anything on the list. Parents worry when this happens. We do not, because it tells us to change the question. We stop asking what the child likes and start asking what they can rule out. Each week the child drops one or two areas and says why: too much memorising, not enough building, too much time with people, too little. After a few weeks the same reasons come back, and the reasons point to an area. A child who can say clearly why three areas are wrong is closer to an answer than a child who says yes to everything.

## What career tests can and cannot tell you

Parents ask us about psychometric and career tests more than almost anything else. Some schools run them. Many firms sell them.

A good test is a useful starting point. It tells you where to look. It is strong on motivation: what drives a child, what they avoid. It can confirm a broad fit, or rule a field out.

It is not an answer. A 14-year-old's results reflect what they have seen so far, not what they could come to love. A child can also answer the way they think they should. Parents tell us their child games these tests. We use four tests that overlap on purpose, so a child who games one shows up as a conflict in another. The conflicts are where the conversation starts.

The mistake we see most is taking the report as the verdict. Fill a form, get a PDF, pick subjects off it. We do not work that way, even when a parent asks us to. We also do not retest a child to get a different answer. The tests do not change like that. What changes is what the child does with the areas the test points to.

## When the answer is too broad

"Business." "STEM." "Medicine." "Something with computers."

These are starting points, not answers. Each one hides very different kinds of work.

Business can mean marketing, finance, economics, or running a company. Medicine can mean caring for patients or investigating biological problems in a lab. Computer science can mean building hardware, building models, or using software to solve a problem in another field.

So we reverse the question. Instead of "what do you want to study", we ask "what kind of problem do you want to work on?" One student interested in business is surveying people on why they buy things they do not need. Another is interviewing restaurant owners about what delivery apps cost them. Same broad interest. Now each has real work to show for it.

Universities do the same thing. The UK and Singapore admit to a course, so the child has to know which course. The US gives more room; most students declare a major at the end of the second year. Either way, "business" is too broad to build on.

## When the child is good at many things

Some children are good at everything and have no idea which thing to keep. One Class 9 student we met played piano, played squash, was learning Spanish, drew, and scored well in physics and maths. Her mother asked us what to do with so many interests.

We look for the thread. Piano and squash both depend on timing, coordination and anticipation. Spanish is listening, interpreting and responding. Maths, chess, robotics and detective fiction share patterns, systems and inference. The thread is often more telling than any single activity.

Then we separate two things. What is part of who the child is, and what they will go deep in. Music can stay in a child's life without becoming the plan. "Not everything you do needs to become your career." Keep what the child enjoys. We are not in the business of taking childhood out of children.

Here is how this ended for one student. She called herself a hoarder of interests, and she did not want to give any of them up. We did not ask her to. We asked which one she would go deep in, and we looked at where her work kept going. It kept going to biology. So biology became her main area, and within it drug discovery, which her projects pointed to most clearly. Her other interests stayed in her life. They stopped competing for the plan.

## When the interest is only passing

The test is persistence, not enthusiasm. A child who spends three or four months a year reading 15 to 20 books on wildlife, without being told to, has shown you something. A child who wants football this month because his friends do has shown you something different. Both are fine. Only one is a basis for a plan.

So when a new enthusiasm arrives, do not act on it yet. A Class 8 child who wants to open a second-hand clothes store does not need a business plan. Let the area of interest emerge. Before you pay for a summer course in psychology, check whether there is a basic fit first.

## What you have at the end

One main area of interest, and one or two backups. Not a career. Not a university. An area, held with enough confidence to make the next decisions.

From there the rest follows in order. The area of interest suggests possible majors. Majors suggest universities. Subject choices for Class 11 and 12 follow from that (see "Choosing subjects after Class 10"). So do activities, and what to say no to.

The child should arrive at the answer, not receive it. If the area of interest is seen as imposed by a parent or a mentor, the child will never own it. We say this to parents directly. The process is a partnership with you. You know your child best, and you also have blind spots. So do we. Together it works.

## How the timing changes by class

Earlier gives room to explore. The same steps run faster with less time.

In Class 8 and 9, the work above takes two to three months, at a weekly pace. There is time to try an area, drop it, and try another.

In Class 10, it still fits. Subject choices for Class 11 are the deadline, so the area of interest needs to be clear before the school asks.

In Class 11, the same steps run in four to six weeks. There is less time for experiments, so we test the stated interest rather than start from scratch. Our guide "Is Class 11 too late to start?" covers this.

## Students who found their area of interest

A Class 10 student arrived set on robotics. The tests showed low interest in open-ended investigation, and the student said so himself. Over about eight weeks of conversations, he narrowed eight possible areas to two by his own reasoning. He built small coding projects to test computer science, and ruled it out. He settled on mechanical and mechatronics engineering, and could say why.

A Class 11 student tested mechatronics, data science and design in turn. She ruled each one out for specific reasons. She arrived at biosciences, then narrowed it further with forced choices: whole systems or single mechanisms, products or pure research.

Across the students we have tracked through this work, four in five changed their area of interest, or became clearer about it, between their first conversation with us and their latest. Every student who came to us with no interest at all has since named one.

## What you can start at home this week

Four things, none of which costs money or adds to the calendar.

Watch, do not ask. Note what your child does unprompted over two weeks, and what they avoid.

Use free exposure. Videos, podcasts and open courses cost nothing. Ask your child to tell you about one thing they watched, and what a day in that work would look like.

Ask why, one level down. "Law" might be argument and logic. "Art" might be design. The reason tells you more than the label.

Do not add activities. Exploring is not a new class or a new club.
