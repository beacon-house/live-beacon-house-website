“What kind of activities should my child do to get into a top university?” 

Parents ask us this all the time.

Many arrive with the same picture: good marks, plus sport, plus music, plus a leadership post, plus some volunteering, plus a research paper, plus a summer programme. Tick every box and the child is ready.

That picture is wrong. This guide explains what a strong profile looks like, how to judge the activities your child already has, and what to add, keep or stop.

## The short answer

A strong profile is not a long list of activities. It shows clearly what your child cares about, what they have become good at, and the work behind it.

At Beacon House, we help students find a promising academic direction and develop real depth in it. That direction might be engineering, economics or another broad field. A small number of connected activities, each taken far, gives us more to work with than a collection of superficial experiences.

Not every activity needs to connect. A child can be serious about science and love music. But the emerging academic interest needs enough deliberate attention to become more than something written in the intended-major box.

## Two questions that help explain admissions

A useful way to understand selective admissions is through two questions.

First: can this student handle the work here? Marks, subject rigour and any required tests like the SAT help answer that. Strong academic preparation is essential for serious consideration at highly selective universities. It does not, on its own, get a student picked.

Second: what will this student bring? Intellectual interests, initiative, contribution, character and the experiences behind them help answer that. The reader needs to understand who the child is, with evidence.

The emphasis differs by destination. UK applications place particular weight on preparation for the chosen course. US holistic admissions also consider interests, responsibilities and contributions beyond the intended major.[1][2][4]

Our focus in this guide is developing academic depth. It is an important part of the picture, alongside the other things that matter in a student’s life.

## From an academic interest to evidence

One way a student can answer “what will you bring?” is through an academic interest they have taken seriously: questions they have explored, abilities they have developed and work they can discuss with understanding.

At Beacon House, we start by asking what interests the child and what they have done to explore it.

“I want to study engineering” names a subject. The next question is: what within engineering has caught their attention? Reading, coding, building or investigating a problem can help them discover that.

Over time, a more specific direction may emerge. The aviation student below shows how an early interest became a question he pursued seriously over several years.

## One student, over several years

A student we worked with had been fascinated by World War Two fighter planes since childhood, and was good at coding. Exploring aviation led him to questions about onboard software and safety. His question became: how can technology make flying safer?

He spent three years on that question. Reading beyond the syllabus. A school club pointed at it. A competition in that space. A research project on the problem, then something built from what he learned.

The application told that story in order: a hobby, an exploration, an insight, and years of depth. His academic interest was clear, and the work showed how he had explored it. That is a meaningful profile.

He did not fix the software that flies a plane. A teenager is not expected to solve aviation safety. The value lies in how seriously he approached the question and what he learned along the way.

## The same shape, outside science

The approach extends beyond science. A Class 10 student interested in economics interned at a government housing company. He sat in on site visits and meetings, and learned how land is acquired for public projects. He encountered cases that raised a question about farmers’ long-term outcomes after accepting cash rather than developed land. What explained the difference, and what should the government do?

That became a question he could investigate, rather than a conclusion to assume. An interest, real exposure, an observation, and a question worth researching. The same shape as the aviation story, in a different field.

## Three layers of evidence

At Beacon House, we use three layers to plan and assess academic depth. They help us identify what the student needs next: more exploration, a serious test of ability, or work they can take responsibility for themselves. They are our planning framework, not a list of admissions requirements.

**Layer 1. Go beyond what school asks, in your child’s area.** Read beyond the syllabus. Use free resources such as MIT OpenCourseWare and Khan Academy. Join or contribute to a relevant school club. No paid programme is needed to begin. Use what the family already has access to.

**Layer 2. Put the work to a credible test.** It is one thing for your child to say they are good at something. A meaningful competition result, selection for a demanding programme or an expert’s assessment gives us evidence beyond that claim. Ask what was required, who judged it and how the student performed. Selectivity matters when we are assessing a selection or award; informed feedback can also help establish the quality of the work.

**Layer 3. Investigate a real question and do something with the learning.** By research here, we mean a structured investigation of a question: examining evidence, using an appropriate method and drawing conclusions the student can explain and defend. The next step may be a project, a design, an essay, a product, a small venture or a programme that runs. Choose the form to suit the question. Our guide “Research or a capstone project?” covers these routes.

The student must own their contribution. Can they explain what they did and why? A mentor can guide and a team can collaborate, but the student must still do the thinking and the work. Ownership can mean taking responsibility within an existing team or project; it does not require starting something new.

One Class 11 student came to us with an impressive robot built mostly by following someone else’s code. We moved him to a mentor who guides rather than builds, so he could take responsibility for work he understood and could explain.

When subject choices, reading, competitions and independent work support an emerging direction, the student can build on what they already know. By Class 12, the essays have work to draw on. The family is not trying to invent a story at the application stage.

Starting early gives room to explore, prepare and try again. Starting later calls for fewer, well-chosen priorities. Our guide “When should college planning begin?” covers the timing.

## What to do with what your child already has

Most children come to us with activities already. Before dropping them or adding more, work out what each one contributes.

**How many activities?** For developing academic depth, we most often reach three or four substantial, connected activities with families, alongside other interests and responsibilities. Common App allows up to ten activities and explicitly says students need not enter ten: the space available is not a target to fill.[3]

**Connect where the connection is real.** A Class 10 student we worked with rode competitively three to four hours a day and was headed for science. The family dropped music and debate to protect his time. Riding stayed, and gave him a route into science: a biogas reactor using horse manure, and a research paper on the carbon footprint of riding. When the mix clogged the pipes, he worked out the ratio himself. The project grew from an interest he already had and gave him scientific work he could explain as his own. Look for connections that lead to real work; interests and service can also matter without an academic link.

**Keep at a manageable level.** A sport your child loves can stay at a few hours a week. It does not have to carry the academic story. Protect time for the work they want to take further, while leaving room for enjoyment and responsibilities at home.

**Cut back where the purpose has gone.** An activity that consumes real hours but offers little learning, contribution or enjoyment is a candidate to reduce. A paper, a capstone and an award in unrelated areas can each be worthwhile. But if all three were chosen mainly to collect credentials, ask whether the child is becoming better at anything through them.

**Say no to new things.** Once the academic direction is clearer, use it as a filter for new academic opportunities. Does this help the child learn something important, test their ability or take the work further? If not, skip it. Saying no is half the work.

## Specific activities, honestly

**Competitions.** Look at the standard, the result and its relevance to the student’s interests. Ten participation certificates tell us little about the level reached. Olympiads can test subject knowledge and problem-solving; building competitions can test design and practical execution. Choose according to what the child wants to develop. A difficult attempt can teach a great deal without a prize, but prestige alone is a poor reason to commit months of preparation.

**Summer programmes.** Ask whether getting in was difficult and what the student will learn. A selective programme can provide external recognition. A programme that admits anyone who pays may still provide useful exposure and learning. Be clear which one you are buying.

**Internships.** The strongest experiences give the child real work or serious exposure, and something specific to explain afterwards. What did they build, investigate, decide or improve? What did they learn? The company name does not make it impressive, and neither does the certificate. A short placement arranged only for the certificate does little.

**Leadership posts.** A title does not make a student a leader. The work behind it does: noticing a problem and acting on it, bringing people together, staying accountable when it gets difficult. Look for that behaviour over time.

**Model UN, debating.** Do it if your child enjoys it. Try a few sessions and see. Do not do it because it sounds impressive.

**A blog or a social media page.** On its own, creating a page tells us little. Writing can show a real point of view, careful thought and growing knowledge. A page about the work can follow the work. It cannot replace it.
