“Which board should we choose for universities abroad? And what if we want to keep India open?” Parents usually ask us this around Class 10, when the decision also involves subjects, schools and a substantial financial commitment.

Our starting point is the university routes the family is considering, the way the child learns and the quality of the schools available. Those three factors lead to a more useful recommendation than asking which board has the best reputation.

## The short answer

**For a family primarily considering universities abroad, we would usually shortlist the IB Diploma and A-levels first**, provided a good school is available and the cost is manageable. Both offer established routes to international university study, with different strengths in breadth, specialisation and independent work. A strong CBSE or ISC school can still be the better choice for a particular student.

**Choose the IB Diploma** if your child wants to continue with a broad range of subjects and is ready to manage independent research, writing and several deadlines alongside exams. The breadth is valuable, but it brings work in subjects beyond the student’s main interests.

**Choose A-levels** if your child has a clearer academic direction and wants to study fewer subjects in greater depth. The combination needs careful thought: narrowing subjects early can rule out courses that require something the student has dropped.

**For a family prioritising JEE or NEET, CBSE would usually be our first recommendation.** In our counselling judgement, the overlap in subject content and the ability to coordinate schoolwork with entrance preparation make it the more practical starting point. Entrance preparation still requires work beyond school lessons.

**If India and overseas options both matter, identify the Indian route you want to preserve.** Intensive JEE or NEET preparation generally points us towards CBSE. If the Indian options do not require that preparation, compare the curricula on the student’s needs and the specific universities under consideration.

**If your child is already doing well in CBSE or ISC, an overseas ambition alone is not a reason to move.** Check whether the current qualification and subjects meet the intended courses’ requirements, then ask what educational benefit a change would bring.

These are BH recommendations about preparation and practical fit. Universities have their own qualification and subject requirements; they do not all apply one common preference between boards.

## What studying each curriculum involves

The comparison matters most for Classes 11 and 12. When parents refer to the “Cambridge route”, IGCSE is usually the qualification taken around Class 10; A-levels follow. Similarly, ICSE is the Class 10 qualification and ISC is the Class 12 qualification.

| **Curriculum** | **What your child takes on** | **What to consider before choosing** |
|---|---|---|
| **IB Diploma** | Six subjects, with three or four at Higher Level and the rest at Standard Level. Higher Level subjects involve more teaching time and greater depth. The core adds an extended research essay, a course examining how knowledge is developed, and commitments involving creativity, physical activity and service.[1] | Does your child want this breadth? Can they manage overlapping assignments and sustained independent work? Are the required Higher Level subjects available and well taught? |
| **A-levels** | Students commonly specialise in three or four subjects. Cambridge International A-level assessment can be staged through AS-levels or taken together at the end, subject to the qualification’s rules.[2] | Is your child ready to narrow their subjects? Does the combination meet the requirements of the courses they may pursue? Ask how each subject is assessed, including any practical work or coursework. |
| **CBSE** | A Class 11–12 subject combination with board examinations in Class 12 and subject-specific practical, project or internal assessment requirements.[3] | Does the school offer the combination your child needs? Can schoolwork, entrance preparation and other commitments fit into a sustainable timetable? |
| **ISC** | A Class 11–12 qualification worth considering alongside CBSE, particularly where the student is already in a strong school. | Examine the actual subject combination, assessment demands and teaching. For an entrance-exam route, compare the school timetable with the preparation required before assuming a move to CBSE is necessary. |

The IB’s additional components create structured opportunities for investigation and writing. A-level specialisation creates more room to concentrate on chosen subjects. Neither guarantees that a student will use those opportunities well. Teaching, feedback and the student’s engagement matter.

For families seeking broad academic degree options, we generally recommend considering the full IB Diploma first. The IB Career-related Programme combines academic and career-related learning and can also lead to higher education, but its acceptance must be checked against the intended universities and courses.[4]

## Check the university options you actually want

A curriculum can suit a child educationally and still fail to meet a particular course’s requirements. Check the qualification, required subjects, subject levels and grades together.

**For US universities, avoid assuming that an international curriculum automatically creates an admissions advantage.** Stanford explicitly states that it has no preference for a specific curriculum, including A-levels, IB, AP or national curricula. It considers the student’s educational context.[5]

**For the UK and Singapore, check the requirements for the qualification your child will hold.** An A-level offer shown prominently on a course page does not tell a CBSE or IB family what their child needs. Find the relevant qualification section and check both overall and individual subject requirements.

Some universities do not accept every school qualification for every course. At the University of Cambridge, for example, CBSE Class 12 is accepted for certain courses at certain Colleges—the constituent colleges within the university. Other applicants may need additional qualifications, such as A-levels or AP exams.[6] If Cambridge is a serious option, check the requirements for the intended course and College before deciding whether CBSE alone will be sufficient.

**For India, distinguish recognition from preparation.** An international qualification may be accepted, but the student must still meet course-specific eligibility and prepare for any entrance exams. Cambridge International’s India guidance makes that distinction explicit.[7]

Your child does not need a final career decision before choosing a curriculum. They do need enough clarity about likely fields and destinations to avoid closing a route they may genuinely use.

## Already enrolled: what problem would a change solve?

Before recommending a move, we establish whether the problem lies in the curriculum, the school, the subject combination or the support the student needs.

**The required subjects are unavailable.** Ask whether this is a board restriction or a school timetable restriction. Another school on the same board may offer the combination. Obtain confirmation of the subjects before making the move.

**Teaching in one subject is weak.** Discuss the problem with the school and assess whether better feedback or targeted support could resolve it. Changing the entire curriculum is a substantial response to a problem confined to one subject.

**A university requires preparation the student’s subjects do not provide.** An additional qualification may sometimes address the gap. Verify acceptance with the intended universities before committing; an extra exam does not automatically replace a missing school subject.

**The student’s needs have changed.** A relocation, demanding sporting schedule or substantial change in academic direction may justify a different school or curriculum. Examine how the proposed arrangement would work in practice, including teaching access and university eligibility.

Doing well is a reason to examine a move carefully, rather than an instruction to stay regardless. Equally, a disappointing term is a reason to investigate the cause before concluding that the curriculum is wrong.

## When is changing worth the cost?

The start of Class 9 and the start of Class 11 are usually the most practical points to consider a change because they often align with the beginning of a qualification. Admission and transfer rules still need checking with the receiving school and board.

At Beacon House, we recommend a change when we can name the benefit: access to necessary subjects, more suitable teaching and assessment, a better learning environment or a workable route to the student’s intended degree.

Set that benefit against the adjustment required. Your child may need to learn unfamiliar writing, research or assessment methods while adapting socially and rebuilding relationships with teachers. One of our students moved from CBSE to the IB in Class 11 and found IB English difficult. The transition created an additional academic demand during an already important year.

Compare the full costs too: fees, transport or boarding, exam charges and any additional academic support. Ask whether that expenditure addresses the need you identified.

A move during a qualification requires an especially clear reason and a confirmed plan for completing the course.

## Would APs or another exam help?

APs are subject exams offered by the US College Board. We recommend them when there is a clear academic purpose or a specific university requirement—not simply because a student follows CBSE or ISC.

An AP might let a student demonstrate knowledge beyond what their school offers. A separate A-level may sometimes address a particular subject requirement. In either case, ask:

- Will the intended universities accept it for this purpose?

- Does it add useful evidence beyond the existing school record?

- Can the student prepare without weakening their school performance?

A strong additional result can demonstrate knowledge. It does not erase a weaker school grade or prove that the earlier result was an exception.

Check registration and result timelines before committing. In India, external students can register through participating AP test centres, subject to each centre’s availability and rules.[8]

Beacon House does not provide AP or subject tutoring. We advise on the academic purpose, choice and timing; preparation sits with the school or a tutor.

## Choose the school as carefully as the curriculum

Before accepting a place, ask who will teach the subjects your child needs, how long those teachers have been there and how the school supports students entering from another curriculum.

Ask to see examples of assignments and feedback. Find out how the school coordinates major deadlines and supports students struggling with the workload. Confirm that the advertised subject combination will actually run.

University destinations provide useful context, but also ask how students with preparation similar to your child’s have progressed. A list of exceptional results tells you less about the support your child will receive.

For a residential school, establish how students can access any external mentors or academic support they may need.

## Two decisions we helped families make

**An overseas ambition did not require a board change.** A family with a strong Class 10 CBSE student asked whether she should move to the IB for Class 11. We recommended staying in CBSE. She was performing well, and changing would mean adapting to unfamiliar teaching and assessment during an important academic period. For her, the stronger plan was to maintain that performance and develop her academic interests beyond the syllabus.

**An additional qualification addressed a specific gap.** A CBSE student considering economics had taken Applied Mathematics, which some of his intended university courses did not accept. He took A-level Mathematics alongside his existing studies. This was a targeted response to a particular requirement, rather than a general recommendation for economics applicants.

Both cases began with the same question: what must change for this student to pursue the courses they want?
