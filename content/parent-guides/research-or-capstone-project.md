“Does my child need a published research paper?” Parents ask us this as early as Class 9. Some assume it is mandatory. Others have been offered a research programme and want to understand what their child would actually do, and whether it is worth the time.

Research and capstone projects now appear on many families’ college-planning checklists. Before committing to either, establish what the student wants to investigate or create, what they are ready to take on and how the work fits with their other commitments.

## The short answer

Your child does not need a published research paper simply to be a strong university applicant.[1–4] At Beacon House, we recommend research when the question, the student’s preparation and the learning justify the commitment.

Research investigates a question using evidence. A capstone brings knowledge and skills together to produce something substantial—for example, a device, an exhibition, a small business or a community programme. A student may benefit from either, both or neither.

Where a student wants to solve a practical problem, we often recommend investigating it before deciding what to build. That investigation can inform the project without becoming a separate paper. For a student exploring history or theoretical science, the investigation itself may be the right work. For a student already committed to demanding activities, improving existing work may be more valuable than adding either.

## What worthwhile research involves

A student begins with a question, chooses an appropriate way to investigate it and develops a conclusion supported by evidence. The method depends on the question. It might involve an experiment, analysis of existing data, interviews or close examination of historical sources.

The intellectual work is deciding what evidence is needed, how reliable it is and what conclusions it can support. A student investigating a policy should be able to explain competing arguments and why the evidence supports their conclusion. A student conducting an experiment should understand what is being measured and what else might explain the result.

The question may change as the work develops. In projects we have guided, students have had to narrow their questions when the necessary data or people were inaccessible. The important judgement is whether the revised question can be answered credibly with the evidence available.

A paper records the question, method, findings and limitations so someone else can examine the reasoning. Writing it is part of learning to make an argument that can withstand scrutiny.

## What makes a capstone substantial?

A capstone gives the student responsibility for developing something beyond an introductory exercise. For a device or app, that includes understanding the problem, building a first version, testing it and revising the design. For a community programme, it includes understanding participants’ needs, delivering the programme and assessing what happened.

We ask three questions before recommending a project: does the problem interest the student enough to sustain the work, can they gain access to the people or resources needed, and is there a version they can realistically complete?

For a project intended for real users, evidence of use and feedback helps establish whether it serves its purpose. For a scientific or technical model, the relevant test may be accuracy or performance. A business may need to demonstrate that customers will pay; an exhibition or community initiative needs a different measure of success.

The project’s ambition should match the student’s preparation and time. A first version that exposes a design flaw can lead to substantial learning if the student investigates the failure and improves it. Choosing a vast problem without a feasible way to test an approach usually leaves them with a proposal instead of work they can evaluate.

## Examples from our students’ work

**Research in biology: how environmental conditions affect protein structure.** One student investigated how environmental conditions affect proteins, using measurements of a liquid’s cloudiness to study changes in the samples. She had to remake samples when measurements proved difficult and examine whether the measurement method supported her conclusions. The work became a paper submitted to the Journal of Emerging Investigators. 

**Research combining environmental science and computing.** Another student investigated dust accumulation on leaves using computer-based image analysis. An environmental question gave him a reason to apply computing methods and interpret the results. The investigation developed into a research paper accepted for publication

**An engineering project: developing a personal tracking device.** A student with robotics experience began building a device to track a person’s location, with the eventual aim of continuing to estimate their position when GPS was unavailable.

**An applied project: teaching children how the brain works.** A student interested in neuroscience developed teaching materials and delivered interactive brain-literacy sessions through three NGOs, reaching more than 50 participants. The sessions used demonstrations and group activities to explain brain functions. Here the output was an educational programme delivered to an audience, rather than a research paper. 

These examples illustrate different forms of work and different stages of completion. None requires the student to claim more than they have actually investigated, built or delivered.

## How we decide whether a student needs research, a capstone or both

First, look at what the student has already pursued. What do they understand well enough to investigate? Which skills do they need to develop? What would this commitment add that their existing work does not?

The choice depends on what would advance the student’s work. For someone developing a robotics device, further testing or competition preparation may be more useful than another paper. A history student may instead develop an argument from original records and historians’ interpretations, with no reason to build a product afterwards.

Schoolwork and existing achievements matter in that decision. An IB student training seriously in sport may have little room for another demanding commitment. Adding research could displace the academic work or training they most need to protect.

Our rule is to choose the work that deserves sustained effort, then give the student enough time to do it properly. A student can have several interests. They do not need to turn every interest into a formal project.

## What a mentor does, and what your child does

A good mentor helps the student frame an achievable question, choose methods and sources, and recognise weak reasoning. They explain unfamiliar techniques and give feedback that requires the student to reconsider or improve the work.

The student should understand the decisions and be able to explain the parts they say they completed. Can they explain why they chose the question, how they investigated it, what went wrong and what the findings mean? If they can only repeat a prepared summary, we would pause and address the gaps in understanding. We have paused projects for this reason.

A parent may arrange access to a mentor. The student’s responsibility is to engage with the question, complete the work and make informed decisions as it develops.

Keep a record of drafts, feedback, methods and results. Where a mentor or collaborator has made a substantial contribution, describe that contribution accurately and follow the publication’s authorship rules. The student should be able to distinguish what they did themselves from what someone taught, provided or completed with them. Harvard and Yale explicitly ask applicants to explain their contribution to collaborative research.[2][3]

Before paying for a programme, ask who will mentor the student, what feedback they will receive, what work they will be expected to do and what happens if the original question proves unworkable.

## When is the right time to begin?

Begin research when the student has read enough to identify a question they genuinely want to investigate, and a mentor can help them choose a method they can understand and carry out. Before committing, establish whether the necessary data, sources, equipment or participants are accessible. If the student cannot yet explain what they want to find out, spend time on reading and smaller investigations first.

Begin a capstone when the student can identify a specific problem or creative aim, explain whom the work is for and propose a first version they can build or deliver with the available guidance. They also need a way to assess it: user feedback, technical testing or an appropriate form of review. A separate research paper is not a prerequisite.

The practical test for both is whether there is enough protected time each week to carry the work through. Plan around school exams, existing commitments and application deadlines. Starting because an application is approaching, without time for failed attempts and revisions, puts pressure on the student to produce an impressive-looking output before they have understood the work.

For a suitably scoped mentored research project, we often plan three to four months from defining the question to a finished paper, with roughly two to three hours of student work a week. These are BH planning estimates; laboratory work, data collection or technical preparation can require a different schedule. Publication, if pursued, adds time after the paper is ready.

For a capstone, agree what completion means before starting: a first working version, a tested and revised product, or a programme delivered and evaluated. Each requires a different timetable.

## Publication: how to judge its value

Publication can give a student the opportunity to have reviewers question their methods and conclusions, then revise the paper. At Beacon House, we help students identify suitable publications and prepare submissions when the work is ready. Acceptance is the journal’s decision.

**Peer review** means reviewers with relevant expertise evaluate the work. Some journals for school students use scientific peer review.[5] Check who reviews submissions, what revisions are expected, the journal’s subject requirements and any fees. The journal’s name alone tells you little about that process.

If a paper is rejected, examine the feedback before deciding whether to revise it or approach another journal. The question may need narrowing, the evidence may be insufficient or the conclusions may go beyond the results. Publication is useful when it reflects a credible review of worthwhile work; acceptance alone is not a reason to choose a journal.

Allow time for review and revisions. The Journal of Emerging Investigators says timing varies with submission volume, the time of year and authors’ revisions.[6] Follow each journal’s submission rules, including restrictions on submitting the same work elsewhere simultaneously. Do not make the application plan depend on a promised publication date.

### Credible publication options across subjects

Look for a named editorial team, reviewers with relevant expertise, clear selection criteria and a record of published work. The following student publications have published submission and review requirements. The right choice depends on the kind of paper your child has written.

| **Area and type of work** | **Publication to consider** | **What parents should understand** |
|---|---|---|
| **Experimental biology, chemistry and physics** | **Journal of Emerging Investigators (JEI)** | Uses scientific review for research that tests a clear prediction. Requires an adult senior author and adult submission. It does not accept every type of science or computing project.[5][7] |
| **Science, engineering, computing, business and social sciences** | **International Journal of High School Research (IJHSR)** | Offers subject-specific journals, including business and social sciences, and describes peer review by researchers and other subject specialists. Check the paper’s fit with the relevant journal.[8] |
| **History** | **The Concord Review** | Selects substantial history research papers written by secondary-school students. Papers must be sole-authored and supported by documented sources. This is a history publication with an editorial selection process.[9] |
| **Literature, philosophy, politics, public policy and related humanities** | **The Schola** | Considers analytical essays by school students, with editorial screening and external review for selected manuscripts. It favours arguments developed from existing scholarship; studies collecting or analysing data, and economics papers relying heavily on mathematical or statistical analysis, generally do not fit.[10] |

Read recent papers and the current submission rules before choosing. Check fees, authorship requirements, review arrangements and expected revisions. A journal that suits an experimental biology paper may be entirely unsuitable for a policy argument or an engineering design. At Beacon House, we help match the completed work to an appropriate publication; the journal’s name is not a reason to choose the project.

Universities differ in how they accept research:

| **University** | **How research can be submitted** |
|---|---|
| **Stanford** | Does not add supplementary research papers or writing samples to the application file.[1] |
| **Yale** | Accepts research supplements. Required application materials receive the most weight; most successful applicants submit only those materials.[2] |
| **Harvard** | Accepts optional research documents, which may receive faculty evaluation at the admissions committee’s discretion.[3] |
| **MIT** | Offers an optional supplement for significant research outside school classes. Students with several projects should focus on the one most significant to them.[4] |

Check each university’s instructions before submitting the work. Where research is included, explain the question, findings and the student’s own contribution clearly.

## What should your family decide now?

Before choosing a programme or mentor, establish what your child wants to investigate or create, what they are ready to do and where the time will come from. If the interest is still broad, begin with reading and practical tasks that help them choose a direction. If a project is already underway, decide whether it deserves more time before adding another.

Our guides “My child doesn’t know what to study. Where do we begin?” and “What does a meaningful student profile look like?” explain those earlier decisions.
