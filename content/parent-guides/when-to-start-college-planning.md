“When should we start planning for college?” Parents of children in Classes 8 to 10 ask us this more than almost anything else. 

Many feel lost the moment Class 9 begins. On the internet, one site says start in Class 8. A friend says Class 11 is fine. Every answer sounds sure, but the answers do not always agree.

This guide gives the recommendation we use at Beacon House, and the reasons behind it. It also explains what belongs in each class, what can wait, and what “starting early” must not mean.

## The short answer

For families considering highly competitive US universities, we recommend beginning in Class 9. Students need time to explore a possible academic direction, develop the knowledge to pursue it seriously and produce work that reflects their own thinking. That development is difficult to compress into the months before applications.

For families focused on the UK or Singapore, begin planning before choosing Class 11 subjects. Check the subjects, levels and grades required for the courses your child may pursue, using the requirements for their curriculum. Then plan the further reading, practical work and any admissions tests around those courses.

A student starting in Class 11 needs a different plan—one that builds on their existing interests and achievements, with sharper choices about what can realistically be developed before applications begin. Our guide “Is Class 11 too late to start?” addresses that situation.

The purpose of an early start is to spread exploration, academic decisions and sustained work across the time available. A plan that fills every spare hour from Class 9 defeats that purpose.

## Why we recommend Class 9

**An interest needs to be tested before it becomes the basis of a plan.** A child who says “engineering” may not know whether they enjoy designing a mechanism, writing software or investigating how materials behave. At Beacon House, we begin with a two- to three-month interest exploration exercise, using reading, practical tasks and discussion to test which academic directions the student wants to pursue further. We look at how the student responds to the work: which questions they pursue, what they find difficult and whether they want to continue once the initial novelty has passed.

**Substantial work requires preparation and revision.** Before a student can investigate a scientific question, they need enough knowledge to frame it and understand the evidence. Before they can build a useful device, they need the skills to design, test and improve it. An experiment may fail; a competition may expose a weakness; an interest may change. Starting in Class 9 leaves room to respond to those experiences and attempt better work.

**Some academic choices need attention well before applications.** A student can become interested in a degree and later discover that they have not taken a required subject. Checking course requirements before Class 11 choices is much easier than trying to repair the omission afterwards. Our guide “Choosing subjects after Class 10” explains what to check.

**The application year is already crowded.** Some applications are due in October or November of Class 12. Choosing universities, preparing essays, arranging recommendations and completing forms must fit around school assessments.

Earlier school marks matter too. Stanford, for example, asks for an official record of subjects and grades covering Classes 9–12.[4] Academic preparation and work outside the classroom need to develop together.

**Examples of application dates for courses starting in 2027**

- **UK:** Oxford, Cambridge and most medicine, dentistry and veterinary courses have a 15 October 2026 deadline. For most other courses, apply by 13 January 2027 to be considered alongside other applications received by that deadline. Some courses may accept later applications if places remain.[1]

- **US:** Harvard and MIT have 1 November deadlines for their early application rounds. Their regular application deadlines are 1 January and 4 January respectively.[2][3]

## What to do in each class

The sequence below explains how we usually plan the work. The examples come from different students in our counselling programme. Similar development can happen through writing, humanities, sport or performance: the student attempts more demanding work, responds to feedback and takes greater responsibility for the result.

### Class 8: build school habits and broaden exposure

At this stage, we would prioritise reliable study habits and opportunities to encounter unfamiliar subjects and activities. A child can try programming, read history beyond the syllabus, learn an instrument or join a team without deciding how it will appear on a university application.

If a school change or subject decision is approaching, investigate it. Otherwise, there is usually little reason to organise Class 8 around formal research projects or intensive admissions-test preparation. For most children in Classes 6 and 7, formal college planning can wait too.

### Class 9: test possible academic directions

Begin with one or two areas the child wants to understand better. Reading introduces the ideas; a practical task reveals whether they enjoy working with them. A conversation with someone in the field can help distinguish the school subject from what studying or practising it involves.

The aim is to reach a better-informed decision about what to pursue next. Enjoying a video about architecture, for example, tells us less than attempting a design and explaining the choices behind it. The early reading, conversations and tasks can often fit into two or three hours a week around school and existing activities.

**An example from our work.** A Class 9 student exploring architecture and engineering designed apartment layouts and a house around a central courtyard. He worked through competing needs: privacy, daylight and air circulation. When his room arrangement left part of an apartment dark, he changed it. He also proposed a fold-down bed with an attached shelf, raising a different question: could the mechanism work as imagined?

The conversations revealed an interest in how spaces and mechanisms functioned, rather than decorative finishes.The initial exercises gave us a better basis for deciding what he should explore next.

Use Class 9 to check the school’s rules for later subject choices as well. Does taking a subject in Class 11 depend on studying it earlier or achieving a particular mark? When are choices finalised, and how much flexibility is there to change them?

### Class 10: deepen the work and settle subject choices

Once an interest has survived the first exploration, choose work that requires the student to use and extend what they have learned. That could mean developing a design, investigating a question or preparing for a demanding competition. Define the question, the skills needed and the intended output before committing months to it.

**An example from our work.** A Class 10 student who competed in equestrian sport connected his interest in biology and the environment with waste from horse stables. He had already built a small working biogas generator as a school project. The next step was to investigate how much gas stable waste could produce.

He read published studies to develop a prediction, designed an experimental setup and planned how to measure gas production. When the available container differed in size from his original design, he had to revisit the quantities and ratios in the experiment. The work was progressing from building a working model to designing an experiment that could test a scientific claim.

Alongside this work, check the exact subject requirements for the university courses under consideration. An interest in a field is not enough if the timetable omits a subject the degree requires. In this student’s case, we advised keeping physics while the longer-term direction was being clarified.

Class 10 is also a board-exam year for many students. At Beacon House, we reduce or pause additional work around exams and plan when it can resume. Protecting academic performance is part of the university plan; a project should not undermine it.

### Class 11: carry demanding work through to a result

By Class 11, a student who has identified a direction should be moving beyond introductory exercises. The challenge is to carry a question or commitment far enough to encounter its difficulties and make decisions about how to proceed.

For a research project, this means examining evidence and recognising what it can support. For an engineering project, it means testing whether the design works. For writing or performance, it means producing work, receiving criticism and improving it. The appropriate standard depends on the student’s experience and preparation.

**An example from our work.** A Class 11 student was developing a computer model to help ride-hailing drivers assess offers. He considered earnings per minute, cancellation risk and whether the destination would leave a driver poorly placed to find another passenger. He had coded these factors; the next task was to test the model using ride records with the fare information his analysis required.

Defining the problem, building the model and testing it were separate stages. Starting before the application year left time to investigate whether his approach worked and revise it in response to evidence.

We would look for a student who can explain the decisions they made, the limitations they encountered and how their work changed as a result. A polished output is less informative if the student cannot explain the thinking behind it. Our guide “What does a meaningful student profile look like?” examines this distinction.

Research is one way to develop this depth. Writing, performance, competitions, sport and community responsibilities can demand sustained learning and initiative too. Universities do not require every applicant to produce a research paper or major project.[5]

Class 11 also brings more demanding schoolwork and, for many US applicants, SAT or ACT preparation. Choose which commitments deserve sustained time and which should stop. By the end of the year, the student should have enough completed or substantially developed work to discuss in applications, even if it continues into Class 12.

### Class 12: make application decisions and explain the evidence

Finalise the university list against the student’s academic record, course interests, budget and other family priorities. Check the requirements and deadlines for each course, including any tests. Allow time for school records and teacher recommendations, as well as the student’s writing. For October–November applications, this work needs to begin well before those months.

The essays should draw on specific experiences. A student describing research should be able to explain the question, method, findings and their own contribution. A student describing a team responsibility should explain a decision they made, the difficulty involved and what followed. Select the experiences that answer each university’s questions and reveal how the student thinks.

Keep schoolwork and established commitments going. Distinguish completed work from ongoing work and future plans. Application writing can make the significance of an experience clear; it cannot supply the learning or achievement that the experience has not produced.

## What changes if the UK or Singapore is the goal?

The planning begins with the degree requirements, before Class 11 subjects are chosen. The application may be two years away, but the subject choices that determine eligibility need attention now.

For the UK, organise further reading and academic work around the subject the student intends to study, and check whether the course requires an admissions test or interview. For Singapore, check the requirements of each university and degree, including how they apply to the student’s curriculum. The tests and application dates may differ from those for the US.

If several countries remain under consideration, plan their requirements together. Work that develops an academic interest may be useful across applications, but it does not replace a required subject or a country-specific test. Decide which options the family wants to preserve before building the timetable around them.

## What should wait until there is a reason to do it?

**Intensive test preparation.** For many students, we plan SAT preparation from the end of Class 10 or during Class 11. Begin with what the student already knows, the improvement needed and the time available around school exams. College Board recommends a first attempt in Class 11 and another in Class 12.[6] Schedule preparation and test dates with room for another attempt if needed, rather than committing a Class 8 or 9 student to years of coaching.

**Paid summer programmes.** Identify the learning the student needs before selecting the programme. Does it offer teaching, facilities or experience that would help them take the next step? A university’s name alone does not answer that question.

**Internships.** Establish what work the student will do and who will supervise it. Observation can help a younger student understand a field; a more prepared student may be able to take on a defined task. Judge the opportunity by those details before treating it as a valuable addition to the plan.

**Research papers.** First establish whether the student has enough knowledge to frame a question, understand the method and interpret the evidence. Reading and smaller investigations can prepare them for that responsibility. Choosing a publication target before the student understands the question puts the decisions in the wrong order.

**More activities.** Examine the commitments already in place. A student may need another six months to improve an existing piece of work, rather than another activity to begin. Add something when you can explain what it contributes and where the time will come from.

## Where does your child stand today?

The next step depends on the gap between the student’s preparation and the decisions ahead. A Class 9 student may need structured exposure before choosing a direction. A Class 10 student may need an immediate subject-requirement check. A Class 11 student may need to stop adding activities and carry their strongest work through to a result.

We understand that this can feel bewildering. Subject choices, academic performance, projects, tests and applications run on different timelines, but they compete for the same student’s time.

At Beacon House, this is part of the planning we manage with families: understanding the student’s direction, checking the academic requirements, deciding which work is worth pursuing and sequencing it around school and application deadlines.

Set up a call to discuss your child’s current preparation, the university options you are considering and the decisions that need attention next.
